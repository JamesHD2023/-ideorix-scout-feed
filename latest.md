1. 💰 Correction: the copyright fee rise is NOT in force, and you have until 5 January

On 24 October this digest told you the US Copyright Office's new fees would take effect "almost certainly as early as 12 November", and said the cheapest week to register a book was that one. The numbers we gave were right. The date was wrong, and here is the correction with the primary document behind it. The Final Rule, titled "Copyright Office Fees", was published in the Federal Register on 18 November, and its own DATES line reads: "Effective January 5, 2027." The 120-day window in which Congress could have objected ended on 11 November, as we said; the Office then set its own commencement date seven weeks later. So nothing has changed yet, and if you have an unregistered book you have about six weeks at the current price rather than the three weeks we gave you, and the deadline we named has already passed without the rise happening. The rule's own old-and-new table: electronic filing for a single author, same claimant, one work, not for hire goes from $45 to $55; all other electronic filings from $65 to $85; paper filing (Forms PA, SR, TX, VA, SE) from $125 to $185. Now the part we did not know in October, and it is the better story. The Office had proposed ABOLISHING the single-application option altogether, on the reasoning that eliminating "this relatively inefficient option" would let it process the common ones faster. Individual creators objected in numbers. The rule records it: "Many individuals opposed this proposal, stating that the Single Application's relatively low fee has been an important cost-saving measure for independent creators." The National Music Publishers' Association warned that "eliminating the Single Application, and thereby removing an affordable option which self-published songwriters and their administrators rely on for crucial cost-savings, could price some creators out of registration altogether." The Office changed its mind: "we are maintaining the Single Application and implementing a modest fee increase from $45 to $55, reflecting an inflationary adjustment." Two more things worth knowing. The Office also pulled back the paper notice-of-termination fee to $275 instead of the proposed $320, saying explicitly that this "responds to commenters' points about the use of this service by individual authors or their heirs". And if you have several works to register, the rule points at the group options, which it says "will continue to provide registration at a fee of only a few dollars per work, in many cases $13 or less". Registration is a US system; authors elsewhere hold copyright automatically, though US registration is open to foreign authors and is what a US court wants to see.

2. ✍️ Keep the claim in the thought and the truth in the hands

Lisa Hall-Wilson at Writers in the Storm, 25 November, on writing a character who is lying to herself — and it is a worked technical post, not a think piece. Her diagnosis of why it goes wrong: writers reach for the author voice to explain what the character cannot see. "She felt..." is the tell she underlines as "a red flag for author intrusion". The cause: "When you collapse what could be four layers into one, you're forced to use the author voice to understand the story." The four layers, as questions to put to a passage: One, what is she thinking about? Two, what is she telling herself is true — "the story the character is telling themselves about what's true", the version they have decided to believe? Three, what do her attention and actions say that she doesn't — "the part the character can't see or won't look at"? Four, and only if the scene needs it, what would it mean about her if the Layer Two story were false? Her fix is to "keep Layer Two running live alongside the action". The five ways she shows a Layer Three leak are the practical core. Where the attention goes: "What a character notices is a confession" — her example is a man who keeps noticing the new director's office, and "You never say he's jealous." Over-justification: "People who are certain don't explain themselves", so the tell is the volume of the defence. Rhythm breaks — short sentences, a topic swerve, a stray detail that does not fit. Action that contradicts the claim: "I'm not angry", and then a drawer closes a little too hard. And other characters not buying it: "That's the fourth time you've checked your phone." One caution she gives herself: her own rewrite is deliberately dense, and "In a full scene, you'd space these out." The line to keep: "Keep the claim in the thought and the truth in the hands."

3. 💼 Read the money like you read a letter

Joe Solari on the ALLi podcast, 25 November, on what profit tells an author that a sales figure does not. The frame: "Profit is a signal from someone else that tells you your creative work has landed in their life." The distinction he spends the episode on is one a lot of indie business talk muddles: "I often see profit and return on investment confused in the publishing industry. Profit is part of the creativity loop. ROI is a measure you use to compare options." His arithmetic, and it is the only arithmetic in the episode: to get return on investment, divide what you earned after costs by what you put in. Spend $10 on an ad and earn $40 and you have $30 profit, a 300 per cent return. Compare a campaign where you spend $5,000 and make $2,000 profit, a 40 per cent return. The second ratio is far worse and the second number is what pays you: "Ratios can tell you what's efficient. They can't tell you what matters." His warning follows from that: "A company or author that lets ROI make every decision slowly stops taking creative risks." On reading readers rather than totals: "For every book, ad, or launch, ask what a reader just told you by buying, passing, finishing the book, or leaving a review", because "Getting one new person to buy your book doesn't mean they've read it or taken another step in their relationship with you." Be told plainly what this episode is and is not. It carries a full transcript and it is mostly philosophy — Drucker, Schumpeter, Csikszentmihalyi — not business arithmetic. There is no profit-per-reader formula, no lifetime-value calculation and no margin target for an indie author in it; the one percentage attached to a real business is in his own bio, describing private clients with gross royalties of twenty-two million in 2023 at an average pre-tax profit of 44 per cent, which is a credential rather than a benchmark for you.

4. 📹 Telling your intuition apart from your fear

The Creative Penn, 25 November: Joanna Penn interviewing Jacqueline Fisch, author of "Intuitive Writing" and "Unfussy Life". We read the episode page and its transcript rather than listening, and the transcript is partial — the back third is not on the page. What is there is worth having. Fisch on what intuition actually is: "I really think it's your body's knowing before your mind can make sense of something." On the confusion that matters: "some people point to their intuition driving them, but what's under it is actually fear. I think that's the important difference to be able to discern: where is this voice coming from?" Her test is physical, and she is cheerfully blunt about it — "our body can't lie to us, which is cool, but also inconvenient sometimes" — so you notice the sensations, and you watch for the moment "you start rationalising and telling yourself a story about it. That's your brain kicking in." Why she bothers: "what we create in a fear-based state... it's just not good", so she calms it first with breathing, feet on the floor, a walk. On rest, which Penn quotes from her book as "Rest is a verb", Fisch's own report is practical: "When I rest and take the time, I have more consistent energy all month long", and "I treat it as a non-negotiable now." On working in seasons: "In the winter, we don't think that the ground is being lazy or unproductive." And on changing her mind in public, which is the part most likely to be useful to anyone who has written a position down: her book had "a section on AI that was the very opposite of how I feel now. I was like, 'Nope, don't touch it.'" What changed: "When I was against it, I also wasn't using it, and it was probably fear." Where she has landed: "I don't need it for a first draft, because I like my drafts to come from me", it is "a tool. It's there to support us", and "I think curiosity is the biggest one." Her fix for having published the old view is simply to publish the new one: "I can talk about that now, and I can release a new edition, or rewrite just that section."

We are giving you the headline and the source and being straight about the rest, because the article itself is now behind a payment gateway — Deadline redirected our fetch to a TollBit URL that answered HTTP 402 Payment Required, and the trade write-up at Screen Daily refused us as well. What we did verify directly: the "MPA" in that headline is Media Partners Asia, a media research firm whose site we reached this morning, NOT the Motion Picture Association, and the two are easy to confuse in a headline. A search this run returned consistent figures from several outlets we could not open to read — global revenue around $20 billion for 2026 with roughly three-quarters of it in China, the money outside China concentrated in four apps each Chinese-owned or Chinese-financed, and thin margins outside China because most of each dollar goes on marketing and app-store fees. Treat those as unverified; we have not read them on a page. The reason to run it at all, for anyone eyeing vertical serialised drama as an opportunity: a market can be enormous and still have no demonstrated route to an income for an individual making their own series, and nothing in any source we could reach this morning described one.
