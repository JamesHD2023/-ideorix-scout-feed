2. 🤖 Amazon now accepts ISNIs, and you cannot do it through KDP

Dan Holloway for ALLi, 26 November, with a development that matters more than it sounds. As of 4 November Amazon accepts ISNIs — International Standard Name Identifiers — for book contributors worldwide. The point of an identifier like this is a clean link between a name and a body of work, which is the problem every author with a common name, a pen name, or a namesake has been living with: the wrong books on your page, your books on somebody else's. Holloway ties it to something this digest has been following all autumn: "the confusion over claims in the Anthropic case", where proving which works are yours turned out to be the whole job. Now the practical part, and it is the reason to read the post rather than ask a chatbot. You CANNOT upload an ISNI through your KDP dashboard. Holloway says so directly, and says Google's AI summary of this story is wrong about it. The routes that do work are Print Retail, Publish to Kindle, Kindle Enterprise Publishing, or indirectly through ONIX if your distributor supports that — which in practice means imprint-based publishers can use the feature now and a plain KDP author cannot. Three things the post does not say, so we are not saying them either: what an ISNI costs, how you get one, and whether this actually cleans up an existing muddled author page. The source it credits is an ISNI press release and a BISG article.

🔗 https://selfpublishingadvice.org/amazon-begins-accepting-isnis/

3. ✍️ Pair the traits, then explain the wound

Angela Ackerman at Writers Helping Writers, 26 November, on manufacturing friction between characters without manufacturing a villain. Three moves. Use a foil — "a character who acts as the opposite of the hero on many levels or in one defining way". Then pair traits rather than pairing virtue against vice: "The obvious choice is to pit one character's attribute against another's flaw", and she suggests the stranger combinations instead — cautious against spontaneous, logical against sentimental, responsible against irresponsible, easygoing against controlling. Then, and this is the part that turns a device into a character, explain the why. Her worked example: Alan, a mechanic newly co-owning a detailing shop, friendly and intelligent but with "a short fuse" that traces to a disapproving father-in-law; a client demands after-hours work on a ding; "The contrasting flaws slam together with a bang, and create conflict." The passage worth keeping is about where oversensitivity comes from: "Painful experiences are hard to forget. Because we have a tendency to fear they could happen again, we're ever watchful for signs. Characters will be, too." On the commercial question, since we flagged this site's paid tool on Wednesday: the craft body here is the bulk of the post and free to read; the promotion is a labelled block at the end offering 35% off a six-month One Stop for Writers subscription and discounts on their thesaurus titles.

4. 🎙️ Where a podcast actually goes in a book proposal

Debra Eckerling in Writer's Digest, 26 November, on podcasting and the platform section — useful if you are writing non-fiction, and we will tell you where it runs thin. The placement advice is concrete. Guest appearances belong in the Author Platform section, with links, because they "boost your credibility" there. If you host a show, "Your podcast goes near the top of your Author Platform section, after your website and newsletter", and you include audience numbers or episode counts if you have them. If you do not have a show, launching one can go in the proposal's Marketing section as a release initiative rather than a credential. On guesting: start with friendly shows, then get "as targeted as you can", research with ListenNotes, and remember that "A smaller, engaged audience may be more valuable than a big show that isn't a fit." Two low-commitment formats she names: a capsule podcast of six to ten episodes, and a live show broadcast to several platforms and then cut for podcast release — "You don't need to commit to a forever podcast." The honest limit: the piece names no thresholds at all. No downloads per episode, no subscriber count, nothing an agent or editor has said they want to see, and no quote from anyone on the acquiring side. So treat it as good advice on WHERE to put the information and not as evidence about how much of it you need.

🔗 https://www.writersdigest.com/podcasting-for-authors-its-role-in-your-book-proposal

5. 📹 How much is a reader worth? One author ran her KDP data through an AI to find out

The Self-Publishing Show, episode 474, posted overnight. Pointer only — we have read the episode listing and its description, not listened. James Blatch and Cissy Mecca on series read-through and what a reader is actually worth to an author business. The description's own account of what she did: Mecca "used Claude to analyse her KDP data across multiple series and pen names for the first time, revealing some impressive read-through rates and identifying a potential problem with a novella in the middle of one of her series." That last detail is the one worth the hour, if the episode delivers on it — a mid-series novella quietly costing read-through is the sort of structural fault you cannot see from a sales graph. Blatch's half, per the description, is the arithmetic: how to calculate read-through, what healthy figures look like, and "why advertising campaigns that appear unprofitable could actually be making you money", plus the difference between organic and paid readers and how international markets change it. There is a free downloadable PDF with step-by-step guidance and an AI prompt for running the calculation on your own data. Sits well beside Wednesday's Joe Solari episode, which argued the principle — profit is a signal from a reader — where this one is the spreadsheet.
