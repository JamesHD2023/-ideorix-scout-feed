1. 🤖 Keeping your drafts will not prove you wrote it — and this digest has told you otherwise three times

Jane Friedman, 27 November, free to read, and it argues against advice we have passed on repeatedly since the middle of October. Start with the two cases she builds it on. An author was dropped by her agent after a UK publisher — "reportedly Cornerstone, part of Penguin Random House UK" — ran her manuscript through the AI detector Pangram. She submitted eleven drafts, character notes and plotting plans. Per Publishers Marketplace, "it wasn't enough to remove suspicion." And Thélyson Orélien, longlisted for the Prix Goncourt, was dropped by his Canadian publisher Les Éditions du Boréal after it found AI use in early versions — where Friedman's reading is that "having early drafts may have in fact hurt his case." Then the argument, and it is the sentence that should stop anyone who has been dutifully saving their working files: "Having process documents helps writers use AI more effectively to produce a commercially acceptable manuscript." A process trail is not evidence of human authorship, because iterative work with a model produces a process trail too. Her further reasons: detection is now granular rather than a single verdict — Pangram will mark seven paragraphs human and the next eighteen not — so a stack of drafts does not address which passages are in question; reviewing process documents "isn't likely to be convincing or definitive either"; and she does not think it helps writers "to take special efforts to create a process trail that they wouldn't ordinarily be able to provide." Underneath all of it: "some agents and publishers don't trust the word of writers any longer." She offers no alternative proof, and says so — the nearest thing to a way out in the piece is a publisher simply asking the author to explain how AI was involved. Our own accounting, because you should be able to weigh it: this digest carried the keep-your-work-materials idea on 18 October (a UK human-certified label requiring work-process materials), on 21 October (a certification mark resting on manuscript history) and on 31 October (an AI policy page whose advice included keeping drafts). We reported those schemes accurately. We did not test whether the thing they ask for actually works, and here is a serious person saying it does not.

2. ✍️ The first 90% takes 90%, and so does the last 90%

Josh Bernoff, 24 November, on the stage of a book nobody talks about. He splits a project into three — ideation and planning, drafting and revising, then preparation for final publication — and his point is that the first two "will get you 90% of the way there" and finishing takes "the remaining 90%", a deliberate steal from software's Ninety-Ninety Rule. The reason it hurts is that the work changes character completely: the creative stages end and a tedious, detail-shaped one begins. His actual checklist, which is the useful part: look for repeated concepts and rationalise them; check that terms are used consistently; find the grammar and spelling errors; footnote the cited material; get the graphics ready. For anything built on a survey he also checks "that the survey questions in the report are rendered exactly as they were asked", and that every chart states its base. On delegating it, he would rather not — if someone else makes the error "you still get blamed", and a helper can change your meaning while tidying your sentences. And a line that connects straight to item 1: finishing is "harder if you used AI at any point in the process", because the model "introduces errors as well as misattribution of ideas", and the finishing stage is where you find them. We ran a version of that argument on 16 October from his survey's respondents; this is him making it himself, with a method attached. His closing image: "You can't get credit for a great gymnastics routine unless you stick the landing."

3. ✍️ Junk food punctuation

Audrey Wick in Writer's Digest, 27 November — a Harlequin author who also teaches English full-time at Blinn College in Texas. Five habits she calls empty calories. One mark used too often, "a semi-colon in every sentence" being her example, because "Placing marks sparingly in your writing helps them feel more rewarding in sentences when they are used." Stacking marks at the end of a sentence — "What message does that send?!?" — when "Crisp sentences end with a single mark." Reaching for bold or italics for emphasis, where the fix is "try choosing a stronger word in the sentence". Non-standard marks, her examples being an ellipsis typed as a row of full stops rather than spaced, and a slash leaning the wrong way. And marks used for each other, a hyphen where a dash is meant. She is careful to say there is "no one-size-fits-all approach" and that genre and form move the norms. It is a short piece and the marks are basic ones, so take it as a checklist rather than a revelation. The self-check she ends on travels further than punctuation: "What's adding bloat to your latest project that doesn't need to be there?"

4. 📣 Who, specifically, reads this?

Ellen Buikema at Writers in the Storm, 27 November, on defining a target audience — with a caution first, since we would rather you knew what you were getting: the piece is general, most of its figures carry no source, and the reader statistics in its worked example are offered as a sample rather than as data. What it does have is a thought exercise worth doing on your own book, and it is better than the usual demographic grid. Having sketched your reader, go and find one: "who do you know who is a 20 – 40-aged individual who lives in a middle-class neighborhood, and enjoys mass-market horror novels? If you personally do not know, ask around." Then ask of that actual person — when did they last read one, do they know why they chose it, can you imagine them reading yours, and if you described your story to them would it make sense to them? The comparable-titles half is the same move pointed at the market: for the books nearest yours, what are the genres, what types of people read them, and how do those authors engage their readers — newsletters, social, Substack? Two places to look that are not Amazon: Goodreads, "which has more of a niche focus", and The Hive Index for online communities; and for graphic novels, "Attend comicons and you will find your readers." The warning she gives is the one most first-time marketers need: "Don't try to write for everybody. Broad audiences make your work difficult to market and sell." No paid course or service is being sold.
