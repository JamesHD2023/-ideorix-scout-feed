1. 🤖 The charter is withdrawn. Eight days, four beats, and the members won

This digest has carried the Society of Authors' Charter of Ethical AI Use since Sunday, and yesterday's item reported the Society defending it. That is now superseded, and the correction is the news: on 21 October the Society of Authors emailed its members to say it has withdrawn from the charter. The Bookseller ran it at 07:45 yesterday morning; Publishers Lunch followed in the afternoon.

The Society's own words, as Publishers Lunch quotes the statement on its website: "To allow time to listen to members' views and concerns, the Society of Authors will withdraw from the Charter." Members are invited to give their views in a survey, and next steps go to the organisation's AGM on 19 November.

The whole arc in one paragraph, because it is a case study in how fast this argument now moves. The charter was published on 13 October with five partner bodies — the Creators' Rights Alliance in the UK, the Authors Guild in the US, the Australian Society of Authors, the Writers' Union of Canada and the New Zealand Society of Authors — and backed by Kazuo Ishiguro and Tracy Chevalier. Authors objected within hours, on two grounds: that there is no ethical way to use generative AI, and that they had not been consulted before their own union published a position in their name. The chief executive Anna Ganley first defended it and apologised for how its purpose had been communicated. Eight days after publication, the Society is out of it.

Two things worth holding on to. The charter itself is still live and no other organisation has withdrawn — so the UK's authors have pulled their body out of a document the Authors Guild and the Australian, Canadian and New Zealand societies are still signed to. And the complaint that actually did the damage was procedural, not philosophical: not "this is the wrong position" so much as "nobody asked us". That is a lesson for any organisation that represents writers, and it is worth remembering next time one speaks for you.

2. 🤖 France has launched a prize for fiction co-written with AI, and it wants your method

AFP, yesterday, 21 October. Three French tech entrepreneurs — Philippe Rodriguez, Albin Serviant and Gilles Mentre — have launched the Prix Pascal, which they claim is the first literary prize for books written with artificial intelligence. There is no jury yet and no declared prize money. Their stated aim is to force the question at other prizes, which are struggling with how to regulate AI use by authors.

Who can enter, in their own words: "The prize is open to anyone who writes in French, anywhere in the French-speaking world, with no requirement of nationality or residence, and without a publisher." What they want: "an unpublished work of fiction, in French, co-written with an artificial intelligence, that dares to do what has never been read before." Submissions close on 30 November, with the award in mid-December. Per The Bookseller's report on 20 October, a five-member jury is due in early November, and Le Figaro reported the writer Jacques Attali was expected to chair it. The entry requirements reported there are striking: a manuscript of 10,000 to 50,000 words, an initial prompt of no more than 100 words, and a technical account of the model and generation process detailed enough that another writer could reproduce it.

The context is the same day's other news. France's literary world has been in uproar since September over allegations that the debut novelist Thelyson Orelien used AI to write his book — it won the FNAC prize in early September before he was accused of cheating and removed from the Goncourt shortlist. After weeks of denials, his original Canadian publisher Boreal said on Wednesday that it had confirmed he used artificial intelligence in the writing process.

And AFP states the unresolved question better than most of the people arguing about it: will authors be allowed to use services such as ChatGPT or Claude for research or writing advice, and when does AI-assisted research become AI writing? Nobody has answered that. Note what the prize is doing in the meantime — asking entrants to document the method so precisely that someone else could repeat it. Whichever side of this you are on, that is the same instrument the human-authorship certifiers are reaching for from the other direction: show your process.

3. 🤖 "Any unsolicited email that starts with flattery is a scam"

Josh Bernoff, yesterday, 21 October, and it lands exactly one day after this digest's item about an author nearly taken in by an approach from a real editor at a real publishing house. His rule, credited to one of his own followers, is the one to tape to the monitor: any unsolicited email or DM that opens with flattery is a scam.

His diagnosis of why it works on us, and on authors especially. We are programmed from childhood to love a compliment; flattery creates instant connection, after which the scammer can begin working you towards paying for something worthless. Authors are unusually exposed because they are, by nature, hoping somebody will say something nice about the book.

His other point is the practical tell, and it is better than the one everyone repeats. Formulaic, fawning flattery is, in his words, an AI tell far more dependable than em dashes. He prints the samples he gets several times a day — messages praising his twenty years at Forrester and his work helping authors communicate complex ideas — and notes what they have in common: every compliment is drawn from information anyone can scrape off a public profile in seconds. His verdict: congratulations on your ability to read a LinkedIn employment history. Anyone can compliment you on what is obvious from your online content, and the recipient is left wondering whether the sender is a fool or wants something.

Then the half that is not cynical, which is why the piece is worth reading rather than just noting. He went and asked his email's AI to find the most notable compliments he has ever received, and quotes a few — a reader who wrote the best email of their life after reading his book, an author who says they would not have been published without him. His conclusion: mild praise that comes from the heart is worth a thousand times the superficial kind, compliments and apologies should never be written by AI, and if you think somebody has done something good, send the note and do not follow it with a request for help.

4. "The only people making money off my books were my cover artist, editor and Facebook ads"

Posted yesterday, 21 October, in answer to a question worth asking out loud: what is the most painful part of self-publishing you have come to accept?

The author's own answer is the most honest accounting of indie economics this digest has carried. Roughly $5,000 spent across their first two books — covers, editing, ads — and fewer than ten copies sold, at an age in their early twenties when the money was not there to spare. Their realisation: the only people earning from their writing were the support people. So they stopped publishing. They did not stop writing: three novels went into the drawer unpublished.

What brought them back is the part to think about before dismissing it as defeat. They learned to design their own covers, refused to pay for ads, and now self-edit, which they say plainly is not ideal but which they are not willing to pay for. Sales are still under ten. The difference is that they no longer finish each book in the negative, and the work is out there.

Two ways to read it, and both are legitimate. One: this is what cutting your costs to fit your sales looks like, and it is a sustainable version of a hobby rather than a business. Two: the first version — $5,000 out, ten copies sold — is what a great many launches actually look like, and the people selling services into that gap do not advertise the median. Set it next to Tuesday's item on paying for editing, where a professional editor argued you should earn your feedback before you buy it.

7. Three new agents building lists, which is the best odds in querying

Jane Friedman's site posted three industry notices yesterday, 21 October. The detail below is what her free summaries carry — the full pieces are behind her paid subscription — and it is enough to act on.

At Massie McQuilkin & Altman, Elijah Wolfson has joined as an agent after two decades in magazine and news journalism. At Triangle House Literary, two new agents: Maya Raiford Cohen, previously an editor at Astra House and Zando, and Alicia Tan, previously at HarperCollins, Abrams, the Today show and BuzzFeed.

Why this is worth five minutes rather than skipping as trade gossip. A newly appointed agent is building a list from nothing, which makes them the most receptive readers in the business for a while — and the backgrounds tell you what each is likely to be hunting. Two decades of news and magazine journalism points at narrative nonfiction and reported books; an editor coming across from Astra House and Zando brings a literary fiction eye and the knowledge of what those houses buy. Check each agency's submission page before sending anything, because a new agent's stated interests usually go up within weeks of the announcement and the notice is not the same as an open call.

8. 🎬 The industry-standard screenwriting program ate hours of a paid assignment

Posted yesterday, 21 October, and it is a warning that travels well beyond screenwriting. A writer on a paid assignment is contractually required to do all the work inside Final Draft's cloud editor, and reports it saving poorly and losing hours of work, freezing constantly, failing at random on cut and paste — which forces a refresh, which loses more hours — and, at the time of posting, refusing to export the PDF. Their summing up of what the hundred dollars bought is not printable here. Their actual question is the fair one: why is this the standard?

Two things to take from it whatever you write in. First, the practical rule: if a tool holds your only copy, you do not have a copy. Any cloud editor you are obliged to use needs a local export at the end of every session, dated, even when the export is the thing misbehaving. Second, the general point: industry standard means widely adopted, which is a fact about history and file formats rather than a promise about reliability. A format everyone can open is worth real money; the editor wrapped around it is a separate question, and this is a working professional discovering they are not the same thing.
