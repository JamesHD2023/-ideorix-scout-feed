1. 🤖 Audible is building AI-voiced overviews of audiobooks, and its announcement does not say whether authors can decline

From Audible's own newsroom post of 16 November, which ALLi picked up yesterday. The feature is called audio discovery: short sound bites that pair "a narrated sample of the audiobook with a brief overview of its themes and what listeners like, plus commentary from an author or Audible editor", offered while you browse and right after a title finishes. Two details are the ones to read twice. The overview is spoken by "Audible's virtual voice, an AI-generated speech technology", and it is assembled from "publicly available material including synopses, metadata and reviews" — so the pitch a listener hears for your book is written from your blurb, your keywords and your reviews, not by you. Author commentary, where it appears, is "drawn directly from recordings of past Audible interviews". It begins rolling out on "select English-language titles in U.S., with plans to expand". What the announcement does NOT say, and we looked: nothing about consent, nothing about an opt-out, and no mention of ACX, self-publishing or independent authors anywhere on the page. Separately, its Ask a Question and Recaps features roll out broadly in December to customers who own the title.

🔗 https://www.audible.com/about/newsroom/audible-personalizes-how-listeners-find-explore-and-return-to-stories

2. No print edition? Your ebook still belongs in the awards

Hannah Jacobson, ALLi's awards adviser, writing yesterday, 21 November, on an assumption that costs ebook-only authors entries they could win. Many competitions accept ebooks in their general categories, some set aside categories or whole programmes for digital editions, and judges are assessing writing and editing — format, as she puts it, is usually a question of logistics rather than merit. The practical part is the bit most authors miss once they have won: an ebook cover can be updated without a new print run, so the seal goes straight on, and it is worth checking it reads at thumbnail size as well as full size. Then the award goes into the book description, the ebook front matter, your bio, and your pitches for interviews and podcasts. The article names no specific awards and points at ALLi's own contest ratings instead, so the shortlisting is still your homework.

3. Build the system, not the willpower

Susan Odev, in Indie Author Magazine on 20 November, starting a second pen name and a second book and deciding to stop relying on memory. Her definition is the useful part: "A system is simply a way of doing something that doesn't rely on memory or willpower." What she built: one project template — manuscript drafts, research, cover files, marketing materials, metadata — duplicated for each new book, which started book two "in minutes instead of hours"; one launch checklist covering preorder setup, ARC distribution, the newsletter sequence, social posts and retailer links; one master document per book holding title, series, blurb, keywords, categories, ISBNs, publication dates, retailer links and cover and editor credits; protected calendar blocks (Tuesday to Saturday afternoons belong to the draft); and backups that mean cloud sync plus regular exports plus copies in more than one place. Her method for choosing what to build next is to start with whatever hurt most this year, and her four questions are worth answering on paper: what cost you the most time or frustration, what one system could you build this week, which tools suit how your brain actually works, and where are you still running on memory or willpower.

🔗 https://indieauthormagazine.com/author-inklings-building-author-systems-that-survive/

4. The fear of being forgotten

Deanna Martinez-Bey, writing for Writer's Digest yesterday, 21 November, on a pressure that gets heavier the longer you write: that the work has to mean something, make an impact, be remembered. Her argument is that permanence was never yours to control — "Books go out of print. Blogs disappear. Magazines fold." — and that chasing it takes time and energy from the only thing you can control, which is the work in front of you today. What she suggests instead is unglamorous and sound: write what genuinely interests you rather than what might impress; mark the small wins, a finished chapter or a scene that finally works; stop measuring your career against anyone else's; let yourself experiment; and remember why you started. Her reframing of legacy is a collection of books, encouragement given to another writer, or one story that helped someone through a bad stretch. "A writer doesn't have to be remembered forever to have made a difference."
