1. A newspaper alleges plagiarism in a 1978 novel, and the author died last year

Reported by the Guardian's Emine Sinmaz yesterday, 22 November, on an Observer investigation. The book is Jilly Cooper's "Prudence", published in 1978 — not "Rivals", though several headlines lead with it because that is what she is known for. The Observer alleges copied passages from two novels: Cyra McFadden's "The Serial", serialised in a US newspaper from late 1975 and published as a book in May 1977, and Josephine Tey's "The Franchise Affair" of 1948. On the counts, the reporting is not consistent and we will not tidy it: the lead says at least 35 passages from "The Serial" while the Observer's detail sets out 15, and the report does not reconcile the two. The Observer's characterisation is "what appears to be extensive and deliberate plagiarism", and on the Tey novel it alleges Cooper "lifts directly" from chapter 22 "both in narrative architecture and in line-by-line choice of words". The paired examples given are small and specific — an ex-husband's "open, loving relationship" becoming an "open living relationship", and a line of advice about cutting out "that nine to five shit". Four things belong beside all of that. These are allegations, not a legal finding. Jilly Cooper died on 5 October 2025, so she cannot answer them. Her agent at Curtis Brown and her publisher Penguin declined to comment to the Observer. And McFadden's daughter Caroline says she is considering legal options — an intention, not a filed case. The investigation began because a reader noticed a similarity.

2. 🤖 The writing machines are good at is the writing that means nothing

Josh Bernoff, on 20 November, with the sharpest reframing of the AI-and-writing argument we have read in a while. His definition of meaningless writing: it has no audience, no decision to support and no judgement behind it. His examples are the five-paragraph school essay and the workplace monthly status update, "just as formulaic". And then the line that does the work: "Since meaning is missing, there's no reason not to use ChatGPT or Claude." His test for the opposite is three-part — meaningful writing "has an audience, a job to do, and a human connection, writer-to-reader" — and in a workplace it enables a specific group of readers to make a decision and carries the writer's own judgement. The practical conclusion he draws is not defensive: set the work that machines cannot do well, which means work where someone actually holds an opinion. His own small examples are a student arguing for the dining halls to stay open late and a worker asking that neurodiverse colleagues' needs be acknowledged in a sales report — meaning does not have to be profound to be real.

3. One name, six genres, and the libraries that buy 25 copies of everything

ALLi's interview with Wendy H. Jones, published yesterday, 22 November, and unusually concrete for the format. A former military nursing officer in Scotland, she writes Scottish crime, humorous and cozy mysteries, YA mysteries, children's picture books and non-fiction for writers — eight books in the Dundee crime series, "about to be nine". The decisions worth copying out. She went indie because a publisher's offer came with "it could be three years before it comes out". She uses ONE name across all those genres, on the reasoning that readers buy her books regardless and in practice cross from one genre to another — which is the opposite of the standard pen-name advice and she gives her reason for it. Her first indie book went into the top 1,000 on Amazon overall and took the number one spot in its categories in launch week. Libraries, she says, buy 25 copies of every book she writes. She placed her picture books with a publisher because "I couldn't do them myself", and her agent handles translation, audiobook, film and TV rights while she self-publishes the books — with a historical novel now going to that agent for a traditional deal. Her business line: treat it as a business, pay for professional editing and covers, and build in contingencies, because a one-person operation stops when the person is ill.

4. How to write an ordinary life in memoir

JI Daniels, in Writer's Digest yesterday, 22 November, against the assumption that memoir needs a catastrophe or a famous name. Four techniques, each with a test attached. Write the people around you as people: memoir "can quickly become an exercise in solipsism, reducing the people in our lives to caricatures", so give them their own private stories and keep a parent complicated — someone who nurtured you and also hurt you. Go back to a memory you think you know and ask what is missing: who else was there, and what do they remember differently? Her own example is a sewing-machine scene that turned, on re-examination, from a family who were hardworking and resourceful into a family who were poor. Replace summary with scene — "My father was difficult, my mother loved me" is true and boring; did he slam doors, or go so quiet that everyone started speaking in whispers? And look for the mundane that reveals: Sedaris on bad jobs and arguments on planes, Dillard on a walk in the woods. None of it is exciting, as she says, except that they made it so.

🔗 https://www.writersdigest.com/how-to-write-an-ordinary-life-in-memoir

💬 Pick a memory you have told often: who else was in the room, and what would they say happened?
