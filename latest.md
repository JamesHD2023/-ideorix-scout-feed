2. Amazon is now selling review requests, and authors are seeing it in their dashboards

Amazon Ads announced Review Requests on 29 September and opened the beta in the US in late October. Its own page describes a campaign that "reaches customers who recently purchased your product and invites them to submit a star rating or written review while they are shopping on Amazon", and sets one hard limit: "Products are eligible when they have fewer than 1,000 total ratings and reviews", with campaigns pausing automatically at that threshold. The eligibility line names "registered sellers and vendors" — not authors. What makes this news now is Dale L. Roberts' report of 19 November: he found the feature live in his own Amazon Advertising dashboard as a KDP author and has launched a campaign on one of his books. He reports a $2 minimum bid, and that you are charged whenever a customer submits a rating or review "regardless of whether it's positive or negative" — a bare star counts the same as a written review. Amazon's claimed result, from its own closed beta in June, is three times as many ratings and reviews per week; that is Amazon's internal data, not an independent measurement. And the obvious observation is Roberts' own: Amazon spent years warning authors never to pay for reviews.

3. 🤖 "AI steals ideas. If you use it, that becomes your problem."

Josh Bernoff, 19 November, on the risk nobody puts in the AI-disclosure debate: not copied words, but uncredited ideas. His argument is that language models gather and rephrase what already exists, so they routinely hand you someone else's framework with no attribution, and "you cannot count on those attributions being accurate" when they do supply one. His test for whether an idea belongs to somebody is practical: did a person set out its justification and details in an article or book, were they first, and did they name the framework. His own example is disarming — he coined "ghostediting", then found John Robin had used it in 2025 and credited him. The checks he recommends are all cheap: search the web before you publish, verify every citation the tool gives you, cross-check with a different tool, and credit the originator with a link for anything recent. The line that should stick: "'I used AI' is not a viable excuse", because your name goes on the book.

🔗 https://bernoff.com/blog/ai-steals-ideas-if-you-use-it-that-becomes-your-problem

4. An agent on why a "nice" query is almost always a pass

Jessica Berg of Rosecliff Literary, writing for Writer's Digest on 19 November, on the single commonest fault in her inbox. "Nice", in her definition, means bland and unexplained: if she can call a query nice, she usually has no idea what the book is — she cannot say who the main character is, what they want, or what happens. She calls that "a composite of the book without any specificity", and it earns a pass because nothing gives her a reason to read the synopsis or the pages. Her remedy is to stop sanding the edges off: lead with the collision in your specific plot rather than its theme, be odd without being gimmicky, and give her a character, a clock and a question she needs answered. Keep the comps current. Say the strange thing in your life that led you to write this book. Her distinction, which is worth copying out: "Kind is generous. Nice is invisible."

🔗 https://www.writersdigest.com/agent-in-your-pocket-why-nice-wont-get-you-signed-and-what-to-aim-for-instead

💬 Read your own opening line as a stranger would: can they name your character, their problem, and the clock?


5. One chapter of a memoir, written in the third person, as a bridge

Jen Murphy Parker, on Writer's Digest overnight, on a structural problem every memoirist with a hard middle will recognise: the book opens low and ends high, and the two halves have to be joined. Her solution in "Fault Line Boy" was a single chapter — placed in the true middle, titled "The Bridge" — that recaps the dark years in the third person, at a remove, told as a story. Two reasons she gives, and both are interesting. It mirrors something true: living with a frightening diagnosis makes you watch yourself from outside, and "you can lose your 'I'." And it quietly reminds the reader that this is a story, not a definitive account of a life. The book then returns to the first person, with her son taking more of the telling through dialogue — so the voice change earns its way back. Her closing claim: "the least personal narrative style allows the reader deeper into the story."
