1. 🤖 Spotify's free audiobook tools reach the UK, Australia, Ireland, Canada and sixteen more

Publishing Perspectives yesterday, 20 November, on Spotify's announcement of 18 November: the free audiobook creation tools in Spotify for Authors have opened in 21 new territories. The list matters more than the number, because most of this community lives in it — the UK, Canada, Australia, Ireland and New Zealand, plus Spain, Mexico, Colombia, Chile, Peru, Ecuador, Guatemala, Bolivia, the Dominican Republic, Honduras, Paraguay, El Salvador, Nicaragua, Costa Rica, Panama and Uruguay. Spanish-language books are supported, with new Spanish voices. What you get: upload an EPUB, choose an AI voice from ElevenLabs, set pronunciations, approve and publish. Five audiobooks free per eligible author, and you need a validated payment profile before the tools unlock. Every title made this way carries a "Digital Voice" label. Two things worth knowing beyond the headline: Spotify says the file is yours and can be published anywhere else, and its FAQ says submitted manuscripts and audiobooks are not used to train generative AI models. Human-narrated uploads still go through the same door. Spotify's own figures: author registrations have more than doubled since the US launch, and over 60% of uploaded manuscripts reach publication.

2. AO3 is back, three features are still off, and your drafts start deleting again on the 29th

Straight from the Organization for Transformative Works' own status page rather than coverage of it. The outage began on 29 October and a security incident was detected around the 29th and 30th; the Archive came back on 15 November, about seventeen days later, then went down again on 19 November for what OTW called preventative maintenance and returned at 23:47 UTC the same day. Still disabled: site icons, external work importing, and downloads in any format other than HTML — "these features will be restored." The date to put in your diary if you keep drafts there: draft deletion has been paused and resumes on 29 November, and the 30-day countdown shown on a draft still runs from its original creation date. On the data: OTW says no AO3 data has been deleted and your works, bookmarks and kudos are safe, that it has "no reason to believe that the user database was compromised, though we cannot rule out that possibility", and that the database holds usernames, email and IP addresses and hashed passwords but no real names. Its advice, in its own words: consider changing your password, check your email address is current, and change that password anywhere else you reused it.

4. Three sisters, three narrators, and the misreadings designed first

Stacey Gordon, writing for Writer's Digest yesterday, 20 November, on structuring her debut around three sisters who inherit a Tennessee pearl farm. Her method is more useful than most multi-POV advice because it starts from the wrong end: decide first what each narrator gets WRONG about the others, and make the gap between perception and truth the engine. Each sister is the others' blind spot, and only the reader holds all three views. The voices separate through what each one notices — one reads the big picture and what she can fix, one reads feelings, one reads aesthetics — and through how they sound: certain even when full of doubt, folksy, or cynical. She kept them apart for the first third, then brought them together, with the rule that "every handoff moves the plot forward". She drafted in order, then mapped events and emotional beats in a spreadsheet to see how each encounter shifted the relationships. The parents never narrate at all, so their myth reaches the reader only through the daughters. The two pitfalls she names: parallel stories that stay separate too long, and narrators who repeat events.

5. 🤖 Ghostwriters are being named now — and the argument is that AI is why

ArtsHub carried this from The Conversation on 18 November: Jane Frank and E Jane Stephens of the University of the Sunshine Coast on the end of the invisible ghostwriter. The cases are the evidence. JR Moehringer is publicly known for Agassi's "Open", Knight's "Shoe Dog" and Prince Harry's "Spare". Graydon Carter's 2025 memoir carries "with James Fox" on the title page, and Carter's own acknowledgement says plainly: "Unlike Keith, I write for a living... The writing is mine, but so much of the order and narrative pace were in good part thanks to James." Ariel Levy went uncredited on Demi Moore's book in 2019, credited on the back of Alex Van Halen's in 2024, and named in reviews by 2026 — her line about the Moore book is the whole craft of it: "The book does sound like her, not like me." The UK's Society of Authors has called for ghostwriters to be properly credited; most still work under confidentiality agreements on flat fees with no royalties. The AI part is the authors' argument rather than a measured finding, and it is an interesting one: as machine-written prose gets more persuasive, a named human collaborator may read as evidence of human involvement rather than a threat to authenticity.

🔗 https://www.artshub.com.au/news/opinions-analysis/celebrity-ghostwriters-are-no-longer-secrets-is-ai-a-factor-2880317/
