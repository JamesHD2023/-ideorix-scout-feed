1. 🤖 Australia's AI committee has reported, and the word authors were looking for is missing

The Joint Select Committee on Artificial Intelligence published "Artificial Intelligence – the Australian way" on 24 November, with 46 recommendations. Alice Grundy of ANU, writing for The Conversation and carried by ArtsHub, sets out what it does ask for: transparency from AI companies about their use of Australian content; payment when work is used by someone else for commercial gain; strong penalties for breaking the law, including breaches of technical protections; incentives for commercial deals between large rights holders and collective agencies; and a mechanism to make it easier and fairer for Australian rights holders to be paid by AI companies training large models in Australia. The Australian Society of Authors responded the same day and put its finger on the gap in one sentence: "we note the absence of the word 'licensing' in the recommendations relating to copyright." Chief executive Lucy Hayward: "We are yet to digest the detail of the report, but welcome the stated ambitions to strengthen protections for creators. The devil will be in the detail." The ASA is also unclear whether payment would come through copyright licensing or "via other proposals that in fact weaken copyright protections", and says a recommendation merely to CONSIDER protecting First Nations cultural and intellectual property "does not go far enough". Grundy's own criticism is that the report does not grapple with the harvesting that has already happened, in Australia or abroad. And Senator David Pocock dissented, arguing the government should leave copyright law alone and enforce what exists, with a warning that Australia could become "the copyright equivalent of the Cayman Islands". This digest covered the ASA giving evidence to this committee on 27 October; this is the result.

2. A co-authorship contract that now has AI clauses in it

The Australian Society of Authors has reissued its Model Joint Authorship Agreement, and for the first time it carries clauses about generative AI plus a sample schedule of permitted AI uses. The agreement is for two or more creators who intend to make a work of joint authorship and want their rights and obligations written down: copyright owned jointly, in equal shares or another defined proportion, with customisable schedules of creative and ancillary responsibilities — drafting, funding applications and the rest. It is an editable Word document from the ASA's resources section. Be straight about what we could and could not check: the announcement says the AI clauses exist and names the permitted-uses schedule, but the document is a paid download with a discounted member price, so we have NOT read the clauses themselves and cannot tell you what they say about disclosure between co-authors, ownership of AI-assisted passages or warranties. The reason to flag it anyway is the shape: a co-author who quietly runs your shared chapters through a model is a problem no handshake covers, and somebody has now written the paragraph.

3. Half a million followers wrote her outline, and she never asked them to

Kim Muench in Writer's Digest on 24 November, and the headline oversells it in a way worth correcting, because the real method is better. She did not poll anyone. Her audience of more than half a million across TikTok, Instagram and Facebook "had already been helping me write the outline. Not intentionally, of course" — years of comments, direct messages, group conversations and requests for help. What she did with that is the transferable part. Listen for repetition, and do not clean up people's language too quickly: parents kept saying "I don't know what I'm supposed to stop doing", and that phrasing is the chapter. Look past the most popular question, because the ones people ask privately or with embarrassment often matter more. Test an idea in a video, a newsletter or a class before committing, and watch for confusion, follow-up questions and pushback. Then build from the problem outward, sorting the questions you already have into chapters. Her own limits, stated plainly: "numbers alone shouldn't decide what you write", your job is still to decide what belongs, and "that doesn't mean letting an algorithm write your book". She adds that you do not need hundreds of thousands of followers to do any of this.

4. A secret is only worth having if it is a train wreck waiting to happen

Writers Helping Writers, 24 November, on building a character's secret. The craft in the post is short and good: not all secrets are bad, but in fiction "they should be potential train wrecks waiting to happen", and the test is simple — the more a character stands to lose if the truth comes out, the more tension the secret creates. Two questions to ask of any secret you have given a character: how far will they go to protect it, and what happens when it comes out? A third, for a secret that is also an ability: does it hurt them or help them? Our honest note on the rest: the post is largely an announcement of a Secret Thesaurus on One Stop for Writers, which is a paid subscription platform, currently promoted at 35% off a six-month plan. What an entry there contains, by the page's own description, is the habits a character uses to hide the truth, the fear at the root of the secret, how the silence is costing them, and ideas for raising suspicion — "well over a hundred" of them, with three sample entries unlocked. Useful if you want it; the four questions above are free.

🔗 https://writershelpingwriters.net/2026/11/character-secret-thesaurus/

5. Six years, a father's diaries, and one piece of advice

Graham Farmelo, interviewed by Robert Lee Brewer in Writer's Digest on 24 November about his authorised biography of Stephen Hawking. The research scale is the useful content: about six years from idea to publication, access to Hawking's father's diaries covering most of his son's life and his mother's writings and journals from 1970 to around 2010, and dozens of interviews with family, peers and associates from his school years to the end of his life. The finds he names are the kind that only come from papers nobody has read — Hawking considered becoming an actor or a politician, Robert Graves taught him English for a few weeks, and he spent a night in police custody as an undergraduate, which Farmelo at first did not believe. His one piece of transferable advice, and the interview really does only give one: "Keep a well-indexed store of all the information you discover about your subject", because without it you will forget most of what you found.
