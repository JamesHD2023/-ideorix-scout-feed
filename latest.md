1. 🤖 Eleven EU countries have asked the Commission to write new copyright law for the AI age

Reported by Luca Bertuzzi for Euronews on 17 November, and it reached us through Author Help UK's Monday roundup. Germany, Spain, Poland, Austria, Cyprus, Croatia, Denmark, Estonia, Portugal, Slovakia and Slovenia submitted a note for the agenda of last week's Culture Ministers' meeting, calling on the Commission to legislate on copyright in the age of AI. Their words: "We call on the European Commission to present a corresponding legislative proposal as soon as possible." The specific ask that matters most to a working writer is not new rights but enforcement of an existing one — EU copyright law already lets creators opt out of AI training, and the eleven want swift action to make that opt-out actually enforceable. Two pieces of context on how fast this moves: the Commission's own work programme puts a legislative package covering copyright in the second quarter of 2027, and the same culture ministries made a similar call in May with eight signatures rather than eleven. The Commission also ran a public consultation on copyright and AI earlier this month.

2. A former agent on the ethics change: the problem is not the mystique, it is the charging

Mary Kole, guest-posting on Jane Friedman's site on 18 November, on the agents' association decision this digest tried and failed to cover last week — the revision that lets AALA members charge writers for editorial feedback. Kole left agenting in 2012, by her own account for a plain reason ("I left agenting in 2012 because I left NYC") and has freelance edited since, so she has stood on both sides of the desk. Her objection is precise and worth quoting: "The problem isn't that agents will lose their mystique by becoming freelance editors. It's that they'll be able to charge for it." The conflict she is pointing at is structural rather than moral — an agent who can be paid for feedback has an incentive that an agent paid only on sales does not. She is candid about why the pressure exists too: "agenting is a very difficult way to make reliable money, especially for the first five years or so." She says she has worked with tens of thousands of writers across both roles.

3. Instagram now detects copied content — and writing is not in it

Jonathan Bailey at Plagiarism Today, 18 November, on Instagram's new content protection tools. How they work: matches are found automatically, the creator gets a notification in their activity feed, and the features are then switched on in settings. Once a match is found there are four responses — Track it, Hide it, Add a Credit Link that labels the copy "original by" you, or Release it. Two limits are the whole story for this audience. It is for "eligible creators", with others able to request access, and no further criteria given. And it covers VIDEO and AUDIO only: the piece describes detecting reused video and matching the same spoken words over different visuals, and never mentions text, captions or written work. So if you post poems, excerpts or writing as images or captions, nothing here protects them. Bailey's own assessment of the state of it: Meta is roughly eight years behind YouTube on content matching, cross-platform copying to TikTok or Shorts is not addressed, and the detection is rolling out slowly.

🔗 https://www.plagiarismtoday.com/2026/11/18/instagram-launches-content-protection-tools/

4. Writing a battle scene the way a game master would run it

Brandy Stoker, on Writers in the Storm yesterday, 23 November, borrowing tabletop logic for fight scenes. The sequence: treat the battle as a sequence of time-bound moves, one per participant, and stress-test the tactics by running it more than once with a variable changed. Then go back and impose fog of war — cut the view down to what your POV character could actually see or do, and deal with everything else after the moment has passed. Make the environment a participant, so terrain, cover, weather and line of sight change outcomes. Give magic and stamina real limits, because a resource that never runs out removes the tension. Make a wound change how someone fights. Give every combatant a tactical motive of their own, and an objective beyond killing everyone in the room. Her closing checklist is the part to keep: a win or loss condition that is not simply killing everyone; arena elements that affect movement or sight lines; a distinct role and physical position for each participant; finite resources that will drain; and unexpected failures to watch for. No worked example in the piece — a commenter asks for one — so it is a framework rather than a demonstration.

🔗 https://writersinthestormblog.com/2026/11/writing-fantasy-battle-scenes-from-dice-to-prose/

💬 In your last fight scene, what did the loser actually want — and what did the room do to both of them?


5. Second person, used in two completely different ways

Jen Ferguson, in Writer's Digest yesterday, 23 November, on the point of view most writers are told to avoid. She separates two uses that get confused. The first she calls slant second person: a narrator who cannot look at their subject directly, often because of trauma, as in Claudia Rankine's "Citizen" and one of the point-of-view strands in N.K. Jemisin's "The Fifth Season". The second is direct address to the reader, from Choose Your Own Adventure through to "This Is How You Win the Time War", where it builds mystery and intimacy. Her own novel gives a dead sister five second-person interlude chapters inside an otherwise first-person book. On when it fails, she is blunt: sustained, it jars, because "for many of us, prose in the second person reads as directive". So her advice is small doses, or one strand among several. Two exercises come with it: write a flash piece of 750 words or fewer in second person, and rewrite a section of your novel that is not working in second person just to see what it shows you.

🔗 https://www.writersdigest.com/how-second-person-point-of-view-can-help-your-fiction-shine

💬 Which section of your draft is stuck — and what would it look like addressed straight to "you"?
